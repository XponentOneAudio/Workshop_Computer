Spectrum

The same sound seen a different way: each circle is a bar, placed by how fast it turns (as a multiple of the first, and in Hz at the current pitch) and as tall as its level. Faint lines mark the exact harmonics 1, 2, 3… A bar off its line is inharmonic. The shapes on the left are the time domain, these bars the frequency domain.

What you're looking at

Each circle turns at a steady speed. The circles are nested: each one is pinned to the rim of the one before, so the outermost point is carried around by all of them at once. Its height, followed over time, is the waveform drawn to the right, and it is exactly what the card sends out of Audio Out 1.

The height of one point turning on one circle is a sine wave. So the waveform is a sum of sine waves, one per circle, and that's the whole idea of additive synthesis: build a sound by adding simple pieces.

y(t) = a1 sin(2π f1 t + φ1) + a2 sin(2π f2 t + φ2) + … + a8 sin(2π f8 t + φ8)

Each circle has three settings, and the 8mu's buttons choose which one its faders change:

  • A · Level (a): the circle's radius, how loud that partial is.
  • B · Phase (φ): where on the circle its point starts.
  • C · Frequency (f): how fast it turns. In the middle of the fader, circle n turns exactly n times for every turn of the first: the harmonic series.

Fourier's idea

In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed that any repeating shape can be built this way, from sine waves at whole-number multiples of its repeat rate, given the right levels and phases. That sum is a Fourier series. Eight circles are enough to see it working: pick Saw, then turn Partials from 1 up to 8 and watch the wave sharpen as each circle joins.

A sharp corner needs infinitely many circles, so eight leave ripples near each jump. Adding more circles squeezes the ripples closer to the jump, but the overshoot never shrinks below about 9%: the Gibbs phenomenon.

Fourier series Gibbs phenomenon Fourier transform

Circles on circles, before Fourier

The picture is far older than the maths. Ancient astronomers, most famously Ptolemy, explained the wandering paths of the planets with epicycles: circles riding on circles. With enough of them they could match almost any orbit, which is Fourier's idea seen from the other side. Tick Trail to see the path the outer point draws in the plane.

Does phase matter?

Change the Phase page and the waveform changes shape, sometimes completely. But listen: a steady tone sounds almost the same. The ear mostly hears which frequencies are present and how loud, not how they line up. This is Ohm's acoustic law. It isn't the whole story: phase matters for sharp clicks and attacks, and when partials aren't exactly harmonic their phases drift anyway.

Off the harmonic series

Move a Frequency fader away from the middle and that circle no longer turns a whole number of times per turn of the first. The pattern never quite repeats, so the wave keeps changing, and the sound gains beating or a metallic, bell-like colour. Real instruments do this: struck bars and bells are strongly inharmonic, and even piano strings, being stiff, have their upper partials pulled sharp. Stretch (Y knob) imitates that, moving partial n from n to n1+s times the fundamental.

Two outputs: height and shadow

Audio Out 1 is the outer point's height (the sines). Audio Out 2 is its position across (the same circles read as cosines), a quarter turn behind. Patch both into an oscilloscope in X–Y mode and it draws the trail above, live. Tick Shadow to see Out 2 drawn downwards.

The ceiling

The card makes 48,000 samples a second, so it can't represent anything above half that, 24 kHz: the Nyquist frequency. A partial pushed past it would fold back as a wrong, unrelated pitch (aliasing), so the card fades each one out between 16 and 20 kHz. Play a high note with all eight partials and watch the top circles shrink.

In instruments

The Hammond organ's drawbars are additive synthesis: each one is the level of a sine at a harmonic, like page A here. Some early digital synthesizers, such as the Synclavier and the Kawai K5, worked this way too.

Things to try

  1. One circle. Choose Sine. One circle, one pure tone. Now raise fader 2 on page A: a second, smaller circle appears on the first's rim, turning twice as fast, and the tone gains an octave above.
  2. Build a saw. Choose Saw and turn Partials (X) slowly from 1 to 8. Each new circle adds a harmonic, and the wave gets closer to a ramp.
  3. Odd only. Choose Square: only circles 1, 3, 5 and 7 turn. Compare it with Saw by ear: the square is hollower, because the even harmonics are missing.
  4. Shape without sound. On Square, go to page B and move a few phases. The wave stops looking square, but listen in the browser and it barely sounds different.
  5. All in line. Choose Pulse: every circle the same size and all starting at the top, so once per turn they all point the same way and add up to a spike. Scatter the phases and the spike spreads out.
  6. Out of line. On Saw, page C, nudge fader 2 a little off the middle. The pattern starts to drift and the sound beats. Then try Bell.
  7. Stretch. Switch to the middle, then turn Y up from zero: the upper partials pull sharp, as on a piano. Switch up (harmonic lock) snaps them all back to the harmonic series.

What FM is

Take two sine-wave oscillators. One, the carrier, is what you hear. The other, the modulator, is wired to bend the carrier: to push its frequency up and down. In the circles above, the modulator's height decides how fast the carrier's point turns.

Slowly, that's vibrato: you hear the pitch wobble. Speed the modulator up into the audio range and the ear can't follow the wobble any more. You hear a new timbre instead: the carrier has grown sidebands, new frequencies at the carrier plus and minus whole multiples of the modulator:

y(t) = Σk Jk(β) · sin(2π (fc + k fm) t)

Two sine waves, and a whole spectrum. That economy is why FM ran the best-selling digital synths of the 1980s.

Chowning and the DX7

FM had carried radio broadcasts since the 1930s. In 1967, at Stanford, the composer John Chowning noticed that vibrato pushed fast and deep enough turned into new timbres, and worked out how to control them. His 1973 paper, The Synthesis of Complex Audio Spectra by Means of Frequency Modulation, was licensed by Stanford to Yamaha, which released the DX7 in 1983, with six sine-wave operators per voice. Its electric pianos, bells and basses are all over the decade's records.

Yamaha DX7 CCRMA

Index, Bessel functions and bandwidth

How bright FM sounds depends on the modulation index, β: how far the carrier's frequency swings (the deviation, Δf), measured in units of the modulator's frequency: β = Δf / fm. The sideband levels are given exactly by Bessel functions of β, Jk(β): at small β only the first pair is there; as β grows, energy spreads to further sidebands, and the carrier itself fades and even disappears (at β ≈ 2.405). A useful rule of thumb, Carson's rule, says nearly all the energy lies within fc ± (Δf + fm).

Bessel function Jacobi–Anger expansion Carson's rule

Ratios: harmonic or bell-like

The sidebands sit at fc ± k·fm, so the ratio between carrier and modulator, the C:M ratio, decides whether they line up into a harmonic series. When it's a ratio of small whole numbers the sound is pitched: 1:1 gives every harmonic, 1:2 the odd ones only (sidebands at 1, 3, 5…). When it isn't, as with √2, the sidebands share no fundamental and the sound is inharmonic: metallic, bell-like, clangorous. Sidebands that would fall below 0 Hz fold back up, phase inverted, landing among the others.

Linear or exponential

An analog oscillator has two kinds of FM input. The usual one is exponential, 1V/oct: the modulator moves the pitch, so each volt up doubles the frequency and each volt down halves it. Doubling adds more hertz than halving takes away, so as depth grows the average frequency climbs and the note goes sharp, by a different amount at every depth. That's fine for vibrato, but makes stable FM timbres hard. A linear input adds the modulator to the frequency itself, in hertz, equally up and down, so the average stays put and the note stays in tune.

VCO CV/gate (1V/oct)

Through zero

Linear FM has a limit. Once the deviation is bigger than the carrier's own frequency (depth above 1 here), the frequency would have to go below zero. An ordinary oscillator can't, so it stops at 0 Hz for part of every cycle: the swing is clipped, the average rises again and the tuning goes. A through-zero oscillator carries on into negative frequency: it runs backwards. Watch the middle trace go below the 0 Hz line, and the carrier's point turn backwards on its circle. It stays in tune at any depth, and its spectrum matches the Bessel prediction exactly.

Phase modulation

Phase modulation adds the modulator to the carrier's phase rather than its frequency. Frequency is how fast phase changes, so PM by a sine is FM by its slope, a cosine: the same sidebands, a quarter turn shifted. The differences are practical. In FM the depth sets the deviation, so raising the modulator's frequency lowers the index and darkens the sound; in PM the depth is the index, so the brightness holds as you change ratio or play up the keyboard. And a steady offset in the modulator (from feedback, or from Audio In 1) detunes FM, but only shifts PM's phase, so PM stays in tune. That's why Yamaha's "FM" synthesizers, the DX7 included, actually use phase modulation.

Feedback and the ceiling

An operator can modulate its own phase: feedback. A little bends the modulator's sine towards a sawtooth; a lot tips it into noise. And because deep FM spreads sidebands so widely, it reaches the Nyquist frequency (24 kHz on the card) easily; sidebands beyond it fold back as aliasing. High notes with high depth get gritty for that reason.

Things to try

  1. From vibrato to timbre. Load Vibrato, then slowly raise the Modulator fader. The wobble speeds up, turns rough, then becomes a steady new tone.
  2. Grow the sidebands. On Deep 1:1, choose D (phase) and bring Depth down to 0, then up. Watch the spectrum fill out, and the carrier (the first dot) dip to nothing near β = 2.4.
  3. Linear against through-zero. Still on Deep 1:1, flip between A and C. Below depth 1 they match; above it, the middle trace of A flattens at 0 Hz while C dips below, and A's average pitch drifts.
  4. Exponential goes sharp. Choose B and raise depth: the average pitch readout climbs, and the spectrum's peaks slide off the harmonic lines.
  5. FM against PM. With C or D, raise the Modulator ratio a few steps. Through-zero gets duller (the same deviation is a smaller index); phase keeps its brightness.
  6. Odd harmonics. Load Clarinet: the spectrum has only the odd harmonics. Nudge Modulator fine and they split into a beating, inharmonic cluster.
  7. Struck sounds. Untick Drone (switch to the middle), load Electric piano or Bell and press Trigger. The brightness falls faster than the level, as it does on real struck objects.
