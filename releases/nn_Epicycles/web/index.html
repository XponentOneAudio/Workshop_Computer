Spectrum

The same sound seen a different way: each circle is a bar, placed by how fast it turns (as a multiple of the first, and in Hz at the current pitch) and as tall as its level. Faint lines mark the exact harmonics 1, 2, 3… A bar off its line is inharmonic. The shapes on the left are the time domain, these bars the frequency domain.

What you're looking at

Each circle turns at a steady speed. The circles are nested: each one is pinned to the rim of the one before, so the outermost point is carried around by all of them at once. Its height, followed over time, is the waveform drawn to the right, and it is exactly what the card sends out of Audio Out 1.

The height of one point turning on one circle is a sine wave. So the waveform is a sum of sine waves, one per circle, and that's the whole idea of additive synthesis: build a sound by adding simple pieces.

y(t) = a1 sin(2π f1 t + φ1) + a2 sin(2π f2 t + φ2) + … + a8 sin(2π f8 t + φ8)

Each circle has three settings, and the 8mu's buttons choose which one its faders change:

  • A · Level (a): the circle's radius, how loud that partial is.
  • B · Phase (φ): where on the circle its point starts.
  • C · Frequency (f): how fast it turns. In the middle of the fader, circle n turns exactly n times for every turn of the first: the harmonic series.

Fourier's idea

In 1807 Joseph Fourier claimed that any repeating shape can be built this way, from sine waves at whole-number multiples of its repeat rate, given the right levels and phases. That sum is a Fourier series. Eight circles are enough to see it working: pick Saw, then turn Partials from 1 up to 8 and watch the wave sharpen as each circle joins.

A sharp corner needs infinitely many circles, so eight leave ripples near each jump. Adding more circles squeezes the ripples closer to the jump, but the overshoot never shrinks below about 9%: the Gibbs phenomenon.

Fourier series Gibbs phenomenon Fourier transform

Circles on circles, before Fourier

The picture is far older than the maths. Ancient astronomers, most famously Ptolemy, explained the wandering paths of the planets with epicycles: circles riding on circles. With enough of them they could match almost any orbit, which is Fourier's idea seen from the other side. Tick Trail to see the path the outer point draws in the plane.

Does phase matter?

Change the Phase page and the waveform changes shape, sometimes completely. But listen: a steady tone sounds almost the same. The ear mostly hears which frequencies are present and how loud, not how they line up. This is Ohm's acoustic law. It isn't the whole story: phase matters for sharp clicks and attacks, and when partials aren't exactly harmonic their phases drift anyway.

Off the harmonic series

Move a Frequency fader away from the middle and that circle no longer turns a whole number of times per turn of the first. The pattern never quite repeats, so the wave keeps changing, and the sound gains beating or a metallic, bell-like colour. Real instruments do this: struck bars and bells are strongly inharmonic, and even piano strings, being stiff, have their upper partials pulled sharp. Stretch (Y knob) imitates that, moving partial n from n to n1+s times the fundamental.

Two outputs: height and shadow

Audio Out 1 is the outer point's height (the sines). Audio Out 2 is its position across (the same circles read as cosines), a quarter turn behind. Patch both into an oscilloscope in X–Y mode and it draws the trail above, live. Tick Shadow to see Out 2 drawn downwards.

The ceiling

The card makes 48,000 samples a second, so it can't represent anything above half that, 24 kHz: the Nyquist frequency. A partial pushed past it would fold back as a wrong, unrelated pitch (aliasing), so the card fades each one out between 16 and 20 kHz. Play a high note with all eight partials and watch the top circles shrink.

In instruments

The Hammond organ's drawbars are additive synthesis: each one is the level of a sine at a harmonic, like page A here. Some early digital synthesizers, such as the Synclavier and the Kawai K5, worked this way too.

Things to try

  1. One circle. Choose Sine. One circle, one pure tone. Now raise fader 2 on page A: a second, smaller circle appears on the first's rim, turning twice as fast, and the tone gains an octave above.
  2. Build a saw. Choose Saw and turn Partials (X) slowly from 1 to 8. Each new circle adds a harmonic, and the wave gets closer to a ramp.
  3. Odd only. Choose Square: only circles 1, 3, 5 and 7 turn. Compare it with Saw by ear: the square is hollower, because the even harmonics are missing.
  4. Shape without sound. On Square, go to page B and move a few phases. The wave stops looking square, but listen in the browser and it barely sounds different.
  5. All in line. Choose Pulse: every circle the same size and all starting at the top, so once per turn they all point the same way and add up to a spike. Scatter the phases and the spike spreads out.
  6. Out of line. On Saw, page C, nudge fader 2 a little off the middle. The pattern starts to drift and the sound beats. Then try Bell.
  7. Stretch. Switch to the middle, then turn Y up from zero: the upper partials pull sharp, as on a piano. Switch up (harmonic lock) snaps them all back to the harmonic series.

Connecting
  • The card picks its USB mode at power-up. 8mu plugged into the card: the card reads it directly and this page isn't needed. Computer plugged into the card: the card appears as a MIDI device called Epicycles, and an 8mu plugged into this computer is passed on by this page. After plugging a computer in, power-cycle the module.
  • Use Chrome, Edge or another Chromium browser. Safari and iOS browsers can't do WebMIDI with SysEx.
  • Press Connect card & 8mu and allow MIDI with SysEx when the browser asks.
  • 8mu buttons A-C (or the tabs) choose the page; D loads the next shape. Faders pick up: one does nothing until it reaches the stored value (the dashed line shows where the 8mu's fader is while it waits).
  • On-screen faders: drag, or focus one and use the arrow keys. Double-click resets one (level: off, phase: 0°, frequency: harmonic).
  • With a card connected, Pitch, Partials, Stretch and Harmonic lock follow the module's knobs and switch.
  • Without a card the page works on its own, and Listen in the browser plays it.

Make your own

Draw with the mouse, a pen or a finger. Each stroke is turned into circles as you lift off, and the drawing closes itself, from the end back to the start. Lift and draw again to add more strokes: they are joined in the order drawn, with a straight line between.

The page finds the edges of the dark parts of the picture, joins them into one path (nearest first, with straight jumps between) and finds the circles that draw it. Bold, simple pictures work best: silhouettes, logos, a few thick lines. Sixty-four circles can draw a heart or a bird, but not a face's every detail.

From a drawing to a sound

Picture a pen going round and round a closed shape at a steady pace. Where it is across, and how high, are two signals that repeat every time round. Speed the pen up to hundreds of times a second and each of them is a sound, with a pitch (how many times a second the shape is drawn) and a timbre (what the shape is). The card sends the height from Audio Out 1 and the position across from Audio Out 2.

To make the pen move, the card uses circles riding on circles, as in partials mode. But a drawing needs more of them, and some must turn the other way.

Circles that turn both ways

Each circle turns a whole number of times, k, every time round the drawing: positive anticlockwise, negative clockwise. Its point is a complex number, its position across the real part and its height the imaginary part, so the drawing is a sum of turning arrows:

z(t) = Σk ck e2πi k t   with   x(t) = Re z(t)   (Out 2),   y(t) = Im z(t)   (Out 1)

One circle draws a circle. Two the same speed, one each way, draw an ellipse: look at Ellipse. Partials mode only has circles turning anticlockwise, which is why its trail is always curly.

Complex Fourier series Euler's formula Negative frequency

Finding the circles

The page puts 1024 points evenly along the outline, then, for each speed k from −127 to 127, turns the whole drawing backwards k times while going round it and takes the average. Everything turning at any other speed averages away to nothing; what's left is the circle at speed k, its size and where it starts:

ck = (1/N) Σn zn e−2πi k n / N

This is the discrete Fourier transform. The card has room for 64 circles, so the page keeps the 64 biggest, and sends them biggest first.

Discrete Fourier transform Fourier series Joseph Fourier

How many circles

Turn Circles (X) right down: one circle, and the drawing is a circle. Bring them in one at a time and the shape arrives in order of importance, the broad outline first and the fine detail last, because detail needs fast circles and fast circles are small. Smooth shapes need few: the heart is exactly eight, the trefoil two. Corners need many, and however many there are the line overshoots a little at each corner: the Gibbs phenomenon again.

Gibbs phenomenon Compression

What you hear

Each output on its own is an ordinary repeating wave. A circle turning k times per drawing puts harmonic |k| into both outputs, whichever way it turns, so a drawing made of slow circles (smooth, round) sounds mellow, and one with corners and spikes sounds bright and buzzy. The circles at k and −k share a harmonic, and depending on how they line up they add in one output and cancel in the other. Rotate (Y) changes how they line up, so the two outputs change timbre as the drawing turns, while the pair together, the drawing, stays the same.

Harmonic series Timbre

On a scope

Patch Audio Out 2 to an oscilloscope's X input and Audio Out 1 to its Y, set it to X–Y mode, and it draws the picture, live. People make whole pieces of music this way, drawing with sound. Listening in the browser, the left channel is Out 2 and the right Out 1, the usual way round for an X–Y scope, or a scope app on a phone or computer.

Slow: a drawing as CV

Switch up drops the card seven octaves, to between about one turn every four seconds and sixteen a second. The outputs are then two related LFOs: patch Out 1 to a filter and Out 2 to a panner and the sound moves around the picture. The Computer's six LEDs become a tiny scope: each lights as the point passes near it.

LFO

Bands: an equaliser for a picture

The eight faders group the circles by speed, an octave each: 1, 2, 3–4, 5–8, 9–16, 17–32, 33–64 and 65–127 turns per drawing. Pull the top ones down and the drawing goes soft and blobby, and the sound dull: a low-pass filter. Pull the bottom ones down and only the wiggles are left. The same thing happens on its own at high notes: circles past 16 kHz fade out, so at C7 only the first seven or so are left.

Low-pass filter Equaliser Nyquist frequency

From a picture to a path

A picture isn't one line, so the page has to make it one. It decides which pixels are ink with a threshold (by default Otsu's method, which picks the grey level that best splits the picture in two), traces round the edge of each patch of ink (marching squares), drops the specks, and joins the outlines into one closed path, always on to the nearest. The jumps between outlines are part of the path, so they get drawn too.

Otsu's method Marching squares Travelling salesman

Things to try

  1. One circle, then two. Choose Circle: one circle, and each output is a pure sine. Now Ellipse: a second circle the same speed the other way squashes it, but each output is still a sine.
  2. Two circles, two speeds. Trefoil and Flower are two circles each, at different speeds and opposite ways. Their sum loops round in petals.
  3. Build a heart. Choose Heart and turn Circles (X) up slowly from 1: it is exactly eight circles.
  4. Corners cost circles. Choose Square and do the same: it needs all 64, and the corners stay a little round, with a small overshoot.
  5. Turn it. On Star, move Rotate (Y) and listen to one output alone: its timbre changes as the star turns, though the drawing doesn't.
  6. Blur it. Pull band faders 8 down to 4 one by one. The drawing loses its corners, and the sound its edge.
  7. Draw your own. On Draw, scribble something spiky, then something round, and compare how they sound.
  8. As CV. Switch up (Slow) and patch the two outputs to two things you can hear moving.
