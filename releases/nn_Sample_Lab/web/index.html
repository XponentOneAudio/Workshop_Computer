What sampling is

A microphone turns sound into a voltage that wobbles smoothly. Sampling measures that voltage at regular instants, here 48,000 times a second, and stores each measurement as a number. Play the numbers back at the same rate through a converter and the wobble, and so the sound, comes back.

Two numbers decide how faithful that is. The sample rate sets the highest frequency that survives: half the rate, the Nyquist frequency. Anything above it doesn't vanish but folds back down as a wrong frequency, aliasing, which is why converters filter first. The bit depth sets how finely each measurement is rounded: each bit halves the rounding error, about 6 dB less noise. The card's converters work at 12 bits.

Samplers

Musical samplers record a sound and play it back on cue, usually at many pitches. The Fairlight CMI (1979) and E-mu Emulator (1981) cost as much as a house; by the late 1980s the Akai S-series and MPC put sampling in every studio, and hip hop, house and jungle were built on it. Memory was precious, so early samplers used low rates and few bits, and that grit became a sound of its own: try the Bits and Rate faders.

Speed is pitch

Play the numbers back faster and every wobble happens sooner: the sound is higher and shorter, just as when a tape or record runs fast. Twice the speed is an octave up and half the length. This is varispeed, engine A, and it's how a simple sampler plays notes, so a sample played high is also short and its character shifts (the "chipmunk" effect). Between stored samples it has to guess the values; the card draws a straight line between neighbours, linear interpolation.

Pulling time and pitch apart

To change the length but not the pitch, or the pitch but not the length, the sound is cut into short grains, a few tens of milliseconds each, smoothed at the ends by a window. Each grain is played at the pitch you want, but the grains are taken from the sample at one rate (the speed) and laid down in the output at another (steady), overlapping so they blend. Slow down and grains are taken from nearly the same place repeatedly; speed up and some of the sample is skipped. That's overlap-add, engine B.

The catch is in the joins. Where one grain's waveform meets the next out of step, they partly cancel, so the sound flutters and sounds phasey, and drum hits can double. WSOLA (waveform-similarity overlap-add), engine C, slides each grain a few milliseconds (the Search fader) to wherever it best continues the one before, and the joins line up. Set Search to zero and it is plain overlap-add again.

The phase vocoder

Another family works on the spectrum instead. The phase vocoder takes overlapping Fourier transforms, measuring how much of each frequency is present and how fast its phase is turning, then rebuilds the sound with the frames spaced differently, advancing each frequency's phase so it stays smooth. It handles sustained, pitched sounds beautifully and smears sharp attacks into a characteristic "phasey" wash. It needs far more computing than the card has, so it lives in the Compare panel. Extreme versions, like Paulstretch, turn a song into a slowly evolving cloud.

Granular synthesis

Take the grains further from the playhead, scatter them in time (Spray) and pitch (Pitch spray), and pile up dozens a second, and the result stops being "the sample, stretched" and becomes a texture made from it: a cloud, engine D. The idea goes back to the physicist Dennis Gabor, who in 1947 described sound as made of tiny acoustic quanta, and to the composer Iannis Xenakis; Curtis Roads and Barry Truax made it practical on computers, Truax in real time from 1986. Set the speed to zero and the cloud freezes a moment of sound, which varispeed never could: stopped tape is silent.

Grain size and window

Grain size is a trade. Long grains keep more of the original intact, but repeat (or skip) audibly big chunks, so rhythms stutter. Short grains hide that, but each join adds a little roughness, and below about 20 ms the grain rate itself becomes an audible buzz. The window matters too: square-edged grains click at every join, a smooth Hann curve doesn't. Low notes need long grains: a grain must hold a few cycles of the lowest pitch to keep it.

Things to try

  1. Speed is pitch. Engine A, then move Speed from 1 down to 0.5: an octave down and twice as long. To 0: silence, as stopped tape would be. Below 0: backwards.
  2. Pull them apart. Engine B at speed 0.5: the same length as A at 0.5 (compare with Audio Out 2) but the pitch stays put. Listen to the drums: they flam and double.
  3. Line up the joins. Switch to C, WSOLA: the plucks lose their flutter. Turn Search to zero and it comes back.
  4. Change pitch, keep time. Speed 1, Pitch +7 on C: a fifth up, still in time with Out 2.
  5. Grain size. On B, sweep the grain size: long grains stutter, short ones buzz. Set Window to square and hear the clicks.
  6. Freeze. Engine D, speed 0, a little spray: one moment of the sample, held as long as you like.
  7. Clouds. D, raise Overlap (grains per second), Spray and Pitch spray: the sample dissolves into texture.
  8. Lo-fi. Bits down to 8, Rate down to 8 kHz: an early sampler. Below that, aliasing whistles in.
  9. Compare. Press Render and compare at speed 0.5: in the spectrograms, see the drum hits stay sharp in A, double in B, and smear in the phase vocoder.

Connecting
  • The card picks its USB mode at power-up. 8mu plugged into the card: the card reads it directly and this page isn't needed. Computer plugged into the card: the card appears as a MIDI device called Sample Lab, and an 8mu plugged into this computer is passed on by this page. After plugging a computer in, power-cycle the module.
  • Use Chrome, Edge or another Chromium browser, and allow MIDI with SysEx when asked.
  • On connecting, the page reads the card's sample (a couple of seconds). Send this sample to the card sends the page's instead, taking a few seconds; the card is silent meanwhile.
  • With a card connected, Speed, Pitch and Grain size follow the module's knobs. Experiments then set the faders and engine, and tell you where to put the knobs.
  • The card holds a little over 2 seconds; longer files are trimmed, and silence at the start skipped.
  • The card forgets its sample at power-off. To keep a recording, read it here and press Save as WAV.
